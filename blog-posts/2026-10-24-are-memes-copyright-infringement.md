Are memes copyright infringement? Sometimes, technically, yes. Most memes start with a picture somebody else owns: a film still, an anime frame, a stock photo, a news photo. Copying that picture without permission is the kind of act copyright law covers. Whether it actually counts as infringement depends on what you did with it, where you live, and where you posted it.

Below is how the US and the EU answer the question. This is general information, not legal advice. If you have a real dispute, talk to a lawyer licensed where you are.

---

## Why memes sit in a gray area

Copyright protects the original image whether or not it carries a © symbol. A screenshot from an anime episode belongs to whoever owns the show. The famous Distracted Boyfriend picture was shot by photographer Antonio Guillem and sold as a stock image, and he has said his photos are covered by copyright and by the stock agencies' licence terms, though he also said he would not chase people using it in good faith.

In daily practice, that attitude matters more than the statute. Plenty of rightsholders let memes slide, but that is their choice, and it can change.

## Fair use in the US: the four factors

US law has no meme exception. What it has is fair use, set out in Section 107 of the Copyright Act. The [US Copyright Office's fair use page](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/) lists criticism and comment among the purposes that can qualify, and it describes four factors courts weigh together:

1. **Purpose and character of the use.** Is it commercial or not, and is it transformative, meaning it adds new meaning or a different purpose? Transformative uses are more likely to be fair. Commercial use counts against you but does not decide the case by itself.
2. **Nature of the original.** Creative works such as films, songs, and anime get stronger protection than factual material. Unpublished works get more protection too.
3. **Amount used.** Courts look at how much you took and how important that part was. Taking a small slice can still be unfair if it is the heart of the work.
4. **Market effect.** Does your use replace sales of the original or hurt a market for it, including a licensing market?

The Copyright Office is blunt that there is no formula. No percentage or number of frames is automatically safe. Each case is decided on its facts.

For a typical meme, factor one often helps (a new joke on top of an old image), factor two often hurts (the source is creative), factor three is mixed (you used the whole frame, but one frame of a 24-minute episode), and factor four usually helps, since nobody skips buying a Blu-ray because they saw a reaction image.

## Parody vs commentary: the Campbell v. Acuff-Rose case

The leading Supreme Court case on parody is *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*, decided unanimously on March 7, 1994 ([opinion at Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/510/569)). The rap group 2 Live Crew released a song called "Pretty Woman" that parodied Roy Orbison's "Oh, Pretty Woman." The publisher sued.

Justice Souter's opinion made a few points that still shape how memes are judged:

- A commercial parody can be fair use. The lower court had treated commercial use as presumptively unfair, and the Supreme Court rejected that.
- The more transformative the new work is, the less other factors such as commercialism weigh against it.
- A parody may need to borrow the recognizable core of the original, because that is how the audience knows what is being mocked.
- Criticism that kills demand for the original is not market harm in the legal sense. Replacing the original is.

The opinion also drew a line between parody and satire. Parody comments on the original work itself, so it has a strong reason to copy it. Satire uses the work to comment on something else and, in the Court's words, "can stand on its own two feet," so it needs more justification.

The catch for meme makers: most memes are closer to satire than strict parody. A Distracted Boyfriend meme about tax season says nothing about the photo itself, so it leans harder on the other factors.

## The EU: parody, caricature and pastiche

The EU works differently. There is no open-ended fair use test. Instead, the 2001 InfoSoc Directive (2001/29/EC) gives member states a list of specific exceptions they may adopt. Article 5(3)(k) covers "use for the purpose of caricature, parody or pastiche". Under the original directive this was optional, so national laws varied.

### Deckmyn v Vandersteen (2014)

The Court of Justice of the EU defined parody in *Deckmyn v Vandersteen* (Case C-201/13), decided in September 2014. A Belgian politician had handed out calendars with a cover closely copied from a 1961 *Suske en Wiske* (Spike and Suzy) comic, redrawn to show the mayor of Ghent. The court held that parody is an autonomous concept of EU law with two essential features: it evokes an existing work while being noticeably different from it, and it expresses humour or mockery. The mockery does not have to target the original work. National courts still have to strike a fair balance between the rightsholder and the parodist's freedom of expression.

That last point matters for memes. Unlike the US parody and satire split, an EU parody can use a picture to joke about something unrelated.

### Article 17 of the 2019 DSM Directive

The Copyright in the Digital Single Market Directive (EU) 2019/790 changed the rules for big upload platforms. Article 17 makes these services more directly answerable for copyrighted material users upload. To stop that from wiping out memes, Article 17(7) requires member states to make sure users can rely on the exceptions for quotation, criticism and review, and for caricature, parody or pastiche, when they upload to these platforms. For that context, the exceptions stopped being optional.

Article 17(9) also requires platforms to offer an effective and quick complaint and redress process, with rightsholders' removal requests subject to human review.

### Pastiche gets a definition (2026)

"Pastiche" sat undefined for years. On 14 April 2026, the CJEU's Grand Chamber ruled on it in *Pelham II* (C-590/23), a decades-long fight over a two-second Kraftwerk sample. The court said pastiche evokes existing works while staying recognisably different, and must create an objectively recognisable artistic or creative dialogue with the source. That can be stylistic imitation, tribute, or humorous or critical engagement. Humour is not required. Simply repeating part of a work with no visible creative interaction does not qualify ([EU IP Helpdesk summary](https://intellectual-property-helpdesk.ec.europa.eu/news-events/news/cjeu-grand-chamber-rules-music-sampling-and-pastiche-first-cjeu-hearing-generative-ai-and-copyright-2026-04-24_en)).

The case was about music, but the test reads a lot like a description of how meme templates work. Practical upshot in both systems: add something real, use a single frame rather than whole scenes, and keep someone else's character off anything you sell.

## How platforms handle meme takedowns

In practice, legal theory rarely comes up. Platforms act first. In the US, the DMCA's notice-and-takedown system (17 U.S.C. § 512) lets rightsholders send a notice, and platforms usually remove the post quickly to protect themselves. In the EU, Article 17 platforms have to respect the user exceptions and run the complaint process described above.

Automated matching does not understand jokes. A reaction image can get flagged as a straight copy even when a court would see it as parody.

---

## What to do if you get a takedown notice

1. **Read it carefully.** Note who claims the rights, which post is affected, and whether it is a legal notice or a platform rule warning.
2. **Be honest with yourself.** Did you add a real joke, caption or edit, or did you repost the image as is? A bare repost is hard to defend.
3. **Use the platform's appeal or dispute button first.** In the EU this is the Article 17(9) process.
4. **Know the US counter-notice rules.** Under [17 U.S.C. § 512(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/512), a counter notice needs your signature, identification of the removed material, a statement under penalty of perjury that you believe it was removed by mistake or misidentification, your contact details, and consent to federal court jurisdiction. The platform then has to put the content back no sooner than 10 and no later than 14 business days after receiving it, unless the claimant tells it they have gone to court.
5. **Do not file a counter notice you cannot stand behind.** Section 512(f) makes knowing misrepresentations on either side liable for damages.

## FAQ

### Is it illegal to make memes?

Making a meme is not illegal as such. Using someone else's image without permission can be infringement, but in the US fair use often covers commentary and parody, and in the EU the parody, caricature and pastiche exceptions do similar work. Risk rises when you sell the meme or repost the image with nothing added.

### Can I get sued for posting a meme?

It is possible but uncommon for ordinary personal posts. The usual outcome is a takedown request or a removed post. Lawsuits tend to involve commercial use, such as brands using a photo in ads or merchandise.

### Does crediting the original creator make a meme legal?

No. Credit does not replace permission or a legal exception. It is still good practice, and it can make a rightsholder less likely to object.
