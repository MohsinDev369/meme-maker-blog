Meme formats explained in one sentence: a format is the set of rules a meme follows, separate from the joke it carries. Know the format and you know where the text goes, how many panels there are, and what the audience expects before they've read a word. Most memes you see today fall into six broad families, and each grew out of a specific moment in internet history.

Researchers have tried to sort these too. In *Memes in Digital Culture* (MIT Press, 2013), Limor Shifman identified nine meme genres, among them LOLcats, stock character macros and rage comics ([review at Leonardo](https://leonardo.info/reviews_archive/feb2014/shifman-barber.php)). The groupings below are narrower and more practical. They're built around a question meme makers actually face: which shape should this joke take?

---

## Image macros

An image macro is a picture with text laid on top, classically in white Impact capitals with a black outline, one line at the top and one at the bottom. The term came from the Something Awful forums, where the forum software let users type a short code that expanded into a stored image, the way a macro does in programming ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_macro)).

LOLcats were the first big image macro wave. Their roots go back to 4chan's "Caturday" posts in early 2005, and the site I Can Has Cheezburger? posted its first image in January 2007, the same year these macros spread well beyond their home forums. Later came advice animals, also called stock character macros: one fixed photo with a fixed personality, like Success Kid or Bad Luck Brian, captioned over and over by different people.

**When to use it:** short, punchy one-liners where the image sets the mood and the text does the work. The top line sets up, the bottom line pays off. If your joke needs three sentences, it's the wrong format.

## Reaction images

A reaction image is a picture posted as a reply, standing in for how you feel. There's often no text at all, or the text sits in the post rather than on the image. These descend from emoticons, and early versions show up on Japanese imageboards and 4chan. The "MFW" ("my face when") convention, where a post opens with a description and attaches a face, was in use on 4chan by 2009, according to archived threads catalogued by Know Your Meme.

Anime is one of the richest sources of reaction images because expressions are drawn big and exaggerated on purpose. Frames like [Disappointed](https://www.meme0.com/memes/disappointed-meme) or [Hiromi Thinking](https://www.meme0.com/memes/hiromi-thinking-meme) work without any caption.

**When to use it:** replies, group chats, comment sections. The joke is the context someone else already provided. If you need to add text, keep it to a label.

## Exploitable templates

An exploitable is an image built to be edited. Instead of laying text over the top, you change something inside the picture: what's written on a sign, what a character is holding, what labels sit on the people in the scene. Know Your Meme dates exploitables becoming a regular part of meme culture to around 2006, and the word itself had an Urban Dictionary definition by 2009.

On Meme0, [Guy Holding Cardboard Sign](https://www.meme0.com/memes/guy-holding-cardboard-sign-meme) and [Kishibe Holding a Note](https://www.meme0.com/memes/kishibe-holding-a-note) are straightforward exploitables. The sign or note is blank, and whatever you write there becomes the punchline.

**When to use it:** when the joke depends on placing text in a specific spot in the scene. Exploitables feel more native than macros because the text looks like part of the world.

## Stock-photo memes

Stock photography was shot to be generic, which makes it perfect raw material. Models act out exaggerated, slightly unreal moments for advertisers, and meme makers reuse that staging for something else.

The best-known example is Distracted Boyfriend. Photographer Antonio Guillem shot it in Girona, Spain, in 2015 and uploaded it to Shutterstock. Its first known meme use came in January 2017, and it went truly viral in August 2017 after a version labelled the three people "the youth," "socialism" and "capitalism".

Structurally, most stock-photo memes are labelled exploitables: you put words on the people and objects.

**When to use it:** comparisons, temptations and choices between options. The staged quality is part of the joke, so these suit everyday or workplace humour better than anything emotional.

## Rage comics

Rage comics are short strips built from a shared cast of crudely drawn faces. The first one was posted to 4chan's /b/ board in 2008. It was a four-panel story ending with a zoomed-in face screaming "FFFFFUUUU-," later known as Rage Guy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rage_comic)). Trollface, drawn by artist Carlos Ramirez the same year, became one of the most reused faces, and Ramirez registered it with the US Copyright Office in 2010.

Rage comics were at their most popular in the early 2010s. Today they read as nostalgic, which is its own kind of usable. The panel-by-panel storytelling also lives on in multi-panel anime formats like the [Aizen comic meme](https://www.meme0.com/memes/aizen-comic-meme).

**When to use it:** relatable everyday stories with a setup and a payoff, or ironic throwback humour. Expect younger audiences to read them as retro.

## Caption-over-screenshot formats

This family takes a single frame from TV, film or anime and swaps out labels or subtitles. It's the dominant format for anime memes. [Is This a Pigeon](https://www.meme0.com/memes/is-this-a-pigeon) is the textbook case: the frame comes from episode 3 of *The Brave Fighter of Sun Fighbird*, which aired in Japan in 1991. It spread on Tumblr in 2011 and had a second, bigger run in 2018, when The Daily Dot named it the best meme template of the year ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is_this_a_pigeon%3F)).

The trick is that captions mimic the original subtitle. That makes the character appear to say your line. [Look What They Need to Mimic a Fraction of Our Power](https://www.meme0.com/memes/Look_What_They_Need_To_Mimic_A_Fraction_Of_Our_Power) works the same way.

**When to use it:** misidentification jokes, dramatic overreactions, and anything where a character's line can be rewritten to fit a new situation. ## How to pick a meme format

The families overlap. A Distracted Boyfriend meme is a stock photo used as an exploitable. Success Kid is a stock character macro that people also post bare, as a reaction image. So rather than worrying about the label, ask a few quick questions.

- **Is it a reply to something?** Reaction image.
- **Is the joke one setup and one punchline?** Image macro or caption-over-screenshot.
- **Does the text need to sit inside the scene?** Exploitable.
- **Are you comparing options or showing a temptation?** Labelled stock photo.
- **Does it need more than two beats to land?** Multi-panel comic.

Formats also age at different speeds. Impact-font macros now read as old-school on most platforms, while screenshot captions and labelled exploitables still feel current. ## FAQ

### What are the main types of memes?

The common image meme families are image macros, reaction images, exploitable templates, stock-photo memes, rage comics and caption-over-screenshot formats. Video and audio memes form separate groups.

### What is the difference between an image macro and a meme template?

An image macro is a specific format: text placed over a picture, usually top and bottom. A meme template is any reusable base image, including exploitables, multi-panel layouts and screenshots, that people caption in their own way.

### Why are they called image macros?

The term came from the Something Awful forums, where typing a short code automatically inserted a stored image, similar to how a macro in programming expands into something longer.
