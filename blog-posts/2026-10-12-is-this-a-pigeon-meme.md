The "Is this a pigeon?" meme is one of the oldest anime screenshots still in daily use. A young man in glasses holds out his hand toward a butterfly, and the subtitle underneath asks, with total sincerity, whether it is a pigeon. People now relabel the man, the butterfly and the question to make fun of anyone who gets something confidently wrong.

The scene is older than most of the people posting it. Here is where it came from and how it turned into a template.

## Where does the Is This a Pigeon meme come from?

The frame comes from *The Brave Fighter of Sun Fighbird* (Taiyou no Yuusha Fighbird), a Japanese mecha series made by Takara and Sunrise that started airing in 1991. It belongs to the long-running Brave series of giant robot shows aimed at kids.

The man in the picture is Yutaro Katori. He looks human, but he is an android built by a scientist named Professor Hiroshi Amano, and the alien hero of the show uses that body to live on Earth. Katori knows very little about the planet, so a lot of his early scenes involve him learning what ordinary things are.

The moment everyone knows happens in episode 3 of the first season. Katori spots a butterfly, gets it wrong, and asks if it is a pigeon. In Japanese the line is "Kore wa hato desu ka?" The [Wikipedia entry on the meme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is_this_a_pigeon%3F) gives the same episode and the same context.

## Is it a bad translation?

No. For years people shared the screenshot as a "subtitle fail," assuming a fansubber had botched the line. Kara Dennison, who worked on a fan translation of the show, has said the subtitle is accurate. The character really is asking that question, because the whole joke of his arc is an outsider trying to make sense of Earth.

That matters for how the meme reads. The confusion was written into the script on purpose. The internet just found it funnier without the context.

## How the meme spread

The screenshot first took off on Tumblr. A user posted it on December 6, 2011, and according to [the meme's documented history](https://meme.com/memes/is-this-a-pigeon/md) the post passed 111,000 notes within three years. At that stage it was only a reaction image. Nobody was editing it yet.

It stayed in circulation through the early 2010s. Within days it was being reposted on anime forums. Smosh put it on a list of anime screenshots in November 2012, and on June 27, 2013, BuzzFeed included it in a roundup of subtitles that went wrong. That BuzzFeed post helped cement the "mistranslation" myth.

The second life started in 2018. Around late April, a Twitter user labeled the butterfly "any makeup look without red lipstick" and changed the question to "Is this a bare face?" That was the turn. Once people saw you could label Katori, the butterfly and the subtitle separately, the image stopped being a single joke and became a fill-in-the-blanks format.

From there it moved fast. Mashable called it the new Distracted Boyfriend that year (we wrote up [the Distracted Boyfriend meme origin](https://www.meme0.com/blog/2026-10-13-distracted-boyfriend-meme-origin) separately). Netflix's official account made one about casting adult actors as teenagers. On December 20, 2018, the Daily Dot named it the best meme template of the year, and in November 2019 Thrillist listed it among the defining memes of the decade.

## What the meme means

The format is about misidentification. Someone looks at a thing and confidently calls it something else. The joke lands because the error is usually obvious to everyone except the person making it.

The image has three slots:

- **Katori** is the person or group making the mistake.
- **The butterfly** is the thing being looked at.
- **The subtitle** is the wrong conclusion, always phrased as "Is this a ___?"

A typical example: Katori labeled "me at 2am," the butterfly labeled "a slight noise downstairs," and the subtitle "Is this a murderer?" Another widely shared one swaps in Christopher Columbus pointing at North America and asking "Is this India?"

Some uses are gentle self-mockery. Others are pointed, like an anime fan labeled as Katori, a three-episode filler arc as the butterfly, and "Is this character development?" underneath. The structure handles both tones because the subtitle does the talking.

## Why it still works in 2026

A lot of memes from 2018 have faded. This one hasn't, for a few practical reasons.

First, the image is clean. There is plenty of empty space around Katori and the butterfly, which leaves room for labels without covering faces.

Second, the question form is easy to write. You don't need setup or a punchline in a separate panel. The subtitle is the punchline.

Third, misidentifying things never stops being relatable. Every new trend, gadget or online argument produces someone who calls one thing by another name, and the template is sitting there waiting.

It also helps that it's an anime screenshot. Anime reaction images like [Hiromi thinking](https://www.meme0.com/memes/hiromi-thinking-meme) or [Look What They Need to Mimic a Fraction of Our Power](https://www.meme0.com/memes/Look_What_They_Need_To_Mimic_A_Fraction_Of_Our_Power) carry the same kind of exaggerated expression that reads well at thumbnail size.

## FAQ

### What anime is the Is This a Pigeon meme from?

It's from *The Brave Fighter of Sun Fighbird*, a 1991 Japanese mecha anime by Sunrise. The scene is in episode 3, where the android Yutaro Katori mistakes a butterfly for a pigeon.

### Who is the guy in the Is This a Pigeon meme?

He is Yutaro Katori, an android created by Professor Hiroshi Amano. The show's alien hero lives inside that body, which is why Katori doesn't recognize everyday things on Earth.

### When did the Is This a Pigeon meme start?

The screenshot was first posted to Tumblr on December 6, 2011. The labeled version most people use today took off in 2018, starting with a Twitter post about makeup in late April of that year.
